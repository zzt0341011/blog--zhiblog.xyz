Walking could never match the speed of a car. In less than twenty minutes the two of them stood beneath the old Wutong Apartment on the outskirts of Chenghai City.

Luo Juexing looked up out of long habit. Counting from the left, the third unit held the thickest gray mist—drifting, almost alive. That was the room where people had died. No doubt about it.

“I’ve already spoken with the landlady, Mrs. Xu,” Huang Jiaye said, starting up the stairs. “She’s more than willing to have those filthy things driven out for good.”

Juexing remained where he was. His left hand drifted to the birthmark on his right arm—the mark he had carried since childhood. His mother once told him it was proof he was different, that he was meant for something greater. Only later did he understand the truth: the mark brought a pain no ordinary person should ever feel. It had become the quiet wound he carried every day.

“Hey. What’s wrong?” Jiaye had reached the second floor, noticed the empty stairs behind him, and come back down.

“Nothing… Let’s go up and see.” Juexing forced a thin smile.

Jiaye clapped him on the shoulder. “That’s better. Clear out the weirdness and we might actually get a decent night’s sleep. Speaking of which—what do you want for dinner? My treat—”

In the shadows, a pair of eyes followed their figures. The right eye looked carved into the face, perfectly still. In darkness it resembled the empty eye of a puppet—hollow, and quietly terrifying.

He smiled. Uneven teeth, one black, one white. Anyone who saw him would think he was a monster. And in truth, he was no ordinary human.

Orange had just finished cleaning her room. She tied the trash bag and carried it toward the bin at the end of the corridor. When she opened her door she saw people standing outside the apartment across the hall. Mrs. Xu was unlocking it, muttering under her breath.

After dropping the bag, Orange turned and immediately recognized Luo Juexing.

“Oh—it’s you!” she called, walking over.

Huang Jiaye reacted first. He turned, and the pure, almost unreal beauty of the girl hit him like a physical blow.

“Whoa. Didn’t expect a beauty like this hiding in a place like this. Definitely worth the trip.” He was practically drooling.

Orange only smiled and nodded, then looked at Juexing. “What are you two doing here?”

Juexing did not answer. He stood at the doorway, studying the layout and the atmosphere of the room with careful attention.

“Haha—my friend here reads feng shui,” Jiaye said cheerfully, steering Orange a few steps aside. “If you don’t mind, maybe we can talk? I’m Huang Jiaye, detective with the Chenghai Major Crimes Unit. May I ask your name…?”

He had already forgotten why they had come.

Juexing knew exactly what Jiaye was like and did not bother intervening. He stepped across the threshold.

The moment his foot touched the floor, a cold draft swept over him. The force was not strong, yet the resentment had clearly been gathering for a long time.

“My name is Qingmu Chengzi. It’s a pleasure to meet you, Detective Huang.”  

Orange kept her tone polite while speaking with Jiaye, but her real interest lay with Juexing and whatever he truly was.

“Oh, sō nan desu ka… so you’re from Japan?” Jiaye managed half a sentence of Japanese, clearly trying to impress her.

“Heh, that’s right. I didn’t expect you to speak any Japanese.” Orange smiled.

“Just a phrase or two…” Jiaye answered, then quickly pressed a hand to his chest. Her smile had sent his heart racing.

“Is something wrong with your heart?” she asked.

“No, no. I’m perfectly healthy.”

“Shouldn’t you be helping him?” Orange glanced toward the open doorway.

“No need. This kind of work isn’t something I can assist with. Only a real cursebreaker can handle it.” The words left his mouth before he realized he had said too much.

“A what-breaker?” Orange caught the unusual term at once.

“Ah—I mean, only Teacher Luo can deal with something like this. Haha.” Jiaye forced a laugh and quickly turned his head toward the room. Inside, Juexing stood by the bed, staring at the mattress with complete focus.

Curiosity pulled Orange forward. Juexing’s voice stopped her cold.

“Don’t come in.”  

The words were low, cold, and echoed strangely inside the small space. She remained in the doorway, watching.

Juexing rested his chin in his hand, thinking. After a moment he turned and walked to the threshold.

“Since you moved in, have you heard any unusual sounds?”

“Unusual sounds…” Orange searched her memory. “Yes. For a while I was working late. When I came back around ten, I would hear a faint *shā-shā* sound while walking down the corridor.”

“Like this?” Juexing scraped his fingernail slowly down the wall.

“Yes. Almost exactly like that.”

“That confirms it. Jiaye—go to the station and bring me every file on the people who died in this room. I’m going to speak with the landlady and prepare a few things.”

“On it.” Jiaye turned and sprinted down the stairs toward the car.

Juexing closed the apartment door firmly.

“Do not enter this room. The curse can only be broken at ten o’clock tonight.”

“Curse?” Orange looked at him with open disbelief.

Juexing nodded once. “It may sound dramatic, but please do not touch anything inside.”

His eyes did not look like those of a liar. As a psychologist and a firm atheist, Orange refused to believe in curses. Yet something about his manner made the word settle uncomfortably in her mind.

“I understand. I have no interest in whatever happened in that room. But you…”

Juexing did not stay to finish the conversation. He left for the second floor to find Mrs. Xu. Watching his back, Orange felt that the man walking away was completely different from the one she had met before.

When she turned to close her own door, two deep scratch marks had appeared on the door across the hall. They looked far too real to be imagination. She shut her door, locked it, and sat on the sofa. Strange fragments of memory flickered at the edge of her thoughts. She shook her head.

“Don’t think about nonsense…”

She finished preparing tomorrow’s lecture slides, stretched, and took a bottle of yogurt from the refrigerator. When she glanced up, the clock already pointed to ten.

“Ten minutes to ten.”

Something important tugged at her. She hurried to the door and peered through the peephole. A familiar figure paced back and forth in front of the opposite apartment—Huang Jiaye, looking faintly ridiculous in his anxiety.

So Juexing had not yet returned. Orange made her decision. She opened the door a crack and called softly.

Jiaye turned. “You’re planning to stay in the room? Outsiders aren’t allowed during a curse-breaking.”

“Will you be inside?” she asked.

“Yes. I have to take notes.”

“Then treat me as your assistant. I’ll only watch. I won’t make a sound.”

Jiaye hesitated. At that exact moment Juexing appeared on the stairs, carrying a coarse hemp sack.

“It’s settled,” Orange said quickly. She tied her hair up, pulled on a cap, and slipped on a pair of plain glasses.

“Time’s almost up. We begin.”  

Juexing did not appear to notice her presence at all—perhaps it was already decided that this atheist psychologist would witness something she could never explain.

The door closed. Silence filled the room so completely that even breathing and heartbeats seemed to vanish.

A soft *scritch*. Juexing struck a match and lit a single white candle, placing it on the bed. He stood five paces away and watched his wristwatch. One minute remained before the sound none of them would be able to dismiss.

Somewhere outside, a distant clock tower began to chime. On the tenth stroke, a faint *shā-shā* rose inside the sealed room—steady, rhythmic, repeating. All three of them heard it as clearly as if it were whispering beside their ears.

The windows were shut. The candle flame should have burned straight. Instead it swayed left and right, as though an invisible hand were playing with it.

Ten minutes passed. The sound grew louder, shifting from slow to restless, as if it had finally noticed the three intruders. Earlier that evening Juexing had studied the files Jiaye brought. The first deaths in the room had been a married couple. A year later, a man fleeing emotional trouble had taken his own life here at the same hour. The recent “haunted house murder” had also been a suicide.

Juexing laid four printed sheets on the floor beneath the bed. Then the impossible happened.

An unseen force dragged the papers under the bed. They vanished into the dark space. Moments later the same four sheets slowly reappeared. Juexing bowed deeply, retrieved them, and noticed a string of crimson characters that had not been there before—written in the lower right corner of the sheet bearing a man’s portrait. The distance was too great for Jiaye or Orange to read them.

Next Juexing lifted the candle and signaled Jiaye to help move the bed. The moment the bed shifted, the *shā-shā* sound exploded in volume. On the wall a long vertical mark in the plaster became visible—exactly the height of a standing adult.

*Could someone have been sealed inside?*  

Orange clamped a hand over her mouth. Her scalp prickled; her heartbeat turned irregular. Juexing glanced sideways at her. She immediately pulled the brim of her cap lower.

No one spoke. Words would have broken the necessary silence. From the sack Juexing took a wooden box roughly a meter long, then a chisel and a hammer.

Clear, sharp strikes rang through the nearly sealed room, drowning out the scraping that had come from within the wall. Before long the cement gave way. A sharp, foul odor rushed out. Inside the narrow cavity a complete skeleton lay exposed—white bones still wearing a jade bracelet, perfectly intact. It was easy to imagine how desperately the victim must have clawed at the cement, trying to escape the cold tomb.

Juexing murmured under his breath for nearly a full minute before carefully placing the bones into the wooden box. He closed the lid and affixed a strip of paper that looked like a sealing talisman. The characters written on it were completely unknown to Orange.

They left the room and shut the door. Only then did Juexing release a long breath.

“Bury the bones together with that man. That will end the nightmare.”  

He handed the files and the box to Jiaye, then walked away without looking back. Every time he finished a curse-breaking, he left the scene with the same cold finality.

Orange hurried to the corridor railing. Juexing’s figure was just visible below. She opened her mouth to call out, but the words stuck in her throat.

“I should go too,” Jiaye said, dragging the heavy sack. “You won’t hear that annoying sound anymore.” He said goodbye and descended the stairs.

Orange still could not believe what she had seen. Yet everything had been real—too real. Was it a dream? She pinched her arm hard. The pain answered clearly: she was awake.

When she reached her own door she glanced across the hall once more. The deep scratch marks were gone—vanished as completely as smoke in an old horror film.

### Character Notes (Chapter 3 – Part 2)

**Qingmu Chengzi (Orange)**  
Psychologist. Firm atheist. Sharp observational skills. Her curiosity and refusal to look away pull her deeper into a world she has always denied exists. The events of this night will not leave her unchanged.

**Luo Juexing**  
Works in near-total silence during a ritual. Precise, detached, and absolute once the process begins. The moment the curse is sealed, he leaves without farewell—consistent with every previous case.

**Huang Jiaye**  
Assigned to record the procedure. Still protective of protocol, yet already bending rules for Orange. Serves as the necessary bridge between the ordinary world and Juexing’s work.

**The Residual Presence**  
Not a classic ghost, but accumulated resentment given form through sound, movement, and the manipulation of objects. Once the bones are removed and properly interred, the phenomenon ends.
