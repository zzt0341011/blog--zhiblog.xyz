Juexing walked to the computer, typed rapidly for a moment, then clicked the mouse. Utada Hikaru’s “Eternally” filled the living room, wrapping everything in a thin layer of quiet sorrow.

Orange sat on the sofa hugging a pillow, watching him approach without blinking.

“Still angry?” he asked.

“Not angry. Just uncomfortable.”

“How’s the atmosphere?” He was asking about the song.

“You like Japanese songs? Utada Hikaru… the theme from the drama *Innocent Love*…”

“About Xiao Qing,” he said. “I made a promise with her that it would stay our secret. But since you asked, of course I’ll explain.”

“If you tell me, you’ll be breaking the promise.” Her tone carried a clear note of challenge.

“If it were anyone else I would keep my mouth shut. You’re different.”

“What do you mean by that?” The way he spoke felt strange, as if he had become someone else.

Juexing shifted closer until only two centimeters separated them. His voice turned almost ethereal, broken into fragments:

“If you want to know… you have to give something in return…”

Orange leaned back. He pressed forward until the sofa armrest stopped her. The music seemed to coil around her heartstrings with unfriendly fingers.

“What… are you trying to do?” The closeness felt suddenly oppressive.

“A man and a woman alone in a room. What’s the most likely thing that happens?”

She pressed a hand against his chest. Beneath her palm his heartbeat was steady—far steadier than her own, which had already become irregular under the charged air.

He watched the way she turned her face slightly aside, the flush on her cheeks carrying a quiet, dangerous charm. The longer he looked, the more he lost himself.

“Could you… not look at me like that?”

“Looking this close… I only just realized how truly beautiful you are.” He sat up straight.

“You’re teasing me. Honestly!” She straightened her pajamas. “Fine. Whether you tell me about Xiao Qing or not, I won’t dig anymore. Because I trust you.”

“Actually, Xiao Qing’s matter is exactly what you saw in that letter. Whether she truly exists or not doesn’t matter. What matters is that Zilan will no longer be bullied, and the school will finally take these things seriously. That’s the ending we wanted.”

“Mm. Still… it feels like Xiao Qing has a crush on you.”

“She’s only a child. If even that bothers you, then I really have nothing left to say. For that, I’m going to punish you.” He suddenly lifted her into his arms.

“Hey—what are you doing?!” Her eyes widened in alarm.

“Do you know what time it is? Time to sleep…” He carried her into the bedroom.

“Oh—it is late. Sorry for keeping you up so long. But you’d better behave tonight, or I’ll kick you onto the floor.”

After setting her down he said, “For safety’s sake, I’ll sleep in the guest room.”

“I was only joking!” she called after him.

He paused in the doorway. “Until our relationship is officially settled, it’s better to keep some distance. Good night.”

The door closed softly. He shut down the computer and went into the guest room.

Orange sat at the desk, staring at the box that still rested there. Although he had not given a direct answer, in her heart he remained someone she could trust. Perhaps she had simply been too narrow-minded.

Night deepened. Orange could not sleep, while Juexing slept as if dead to the world. She entered the guest room, drew the blanket properly over him, then pulled a stool close, rested her head on his arm, and finally drifted off while holding his hand.

Juexing opened one eye a fraction and smiled with quiet contentment.

They entered the dream together.

A house stood surrounded by withered trees. The path that wound toward it was quiet, yet the cold atmosphere and the mottled shadows of the branches made the skin crawl.

From the outside the building looked like something from the 1970s—old-style windows, peeling paint, some panes already broken. Wind whistled through the gaps.

Under a sky without moon or stars the entire house felt deeply unnatural. One could not help thinking of the demon castles that appear in films.

The second-floor wall was covered in ivy that glowed a deep, almost blackish green in the desolate night, as if warning any who approached that this was a place of misfortune—think twice before entering.

Not a single light burned inside. Only occasional flashes of lightning illuminated the moss-covered wooden main door.

A sound like electric static—or like television snow—crackled for a moment. Then a long shadow stretched across the great wooden door as a figure approached from the distance.

Someone was running hard along the path, the movement of a person coming to save another.

He stopped in front of the wooden door. He had no idea where he was or how he would leave.

A string of questions hung in the air like unreachable stars. He turned in every direction, yet the surroundings formed a barrier that locked him firmly inside.

No matter which way he walked he returned to the same wooden door. Clearly the only choice left was to push it open and see what lay beyond.

Cold sweat beaded on his forehead and palms. His heartbeat looked calm, yet it could not hide the fear underneath. His hands hovered in front of the door; he could not predict what would happen next. Then a single cry from the second floor—“Help!”—forced him to push the door open.

The front courtyard was sparse earth. Only a short path of loose stones led to the entrance of the ground-floor hall.

He steeled himself and followed the stone path. Behind him the wooden door slowly closed. In that instant he felt something moving beside him—something pushing up through the soil.

He glanced sideways. With every step he took, a tombstone rose from the earth. Whose names were carved on them no longer seemed to matter.

More and more stones appeared. When he looked up at the house now only a few paces away, it had become a gigantic grave—tombstones for a door, massive rock for a body.

He had walked alive into a graveyard, or perhaps a mass burial ground…

He closed his eyes and emptied his mind. All of this was only illusion. These terrifying sights were born from the fear inside him. If the heart was strong enough and the belief firm enough, nothing needed to be feared.

When he opened his eyes again, the house was still only a house—an ordinary 1970s building standing in the pitch-black night—though the crooked tombstones of every size still lined both sides of the path.

He drew a deep breath and slowly pushed open the main door. The aged wood protested with long, grating creaks.

The cry for help had come from the second floor. Though it had been only a single word, it remained clear in his mind.

He did not linger on the ground floor. He went straight upstairs. Standing in the corridor he faced two symmetrical rows of guest rooms—perhaps ten in total. He slowed and lightened his steps until he reached the first door.

He listened. Silence. No sign of anyone inside. Through the thin wood he placed his hand on the handle and turned it to the right. The door opened a narrow crack with a soft *click*.

Inside was absolute blackness—nothing visible, not even the outline of furniture.

He did not enter. The nameless fear that had subsided earlier began to rise again. He pulled the door shut. At that moment he felt someone standing at the far end of the corridor—or rather, a pair of eyes fixed unblinkingly on him. He turned sharply. A strip of white silk fluttered past the far end of the corridor and vanished behind one of the doors. Then the cry for help came again. He was certain now: it was a woman’s voice, calling out in fear and helplessness.

He walked quickly. In a few strides his figure was swallowed by the darkness at the other end of the corridor.

It might be a road with no return. Even knowing the story might have no ending, he still went forward without looking back—because someone had once told him: this is the principle by which you live…

Does choosing a simple principle require a lifetime to protect it?

No one would give him a complete answer. Or perhaps this was precisely the answer everyone keeps searching for and never finds.

Darkness… fear… ugliness…

What danger hides behind these ferocious masks? And once the masks are removed, what will be seen?

He had been completely swallowed by the dark. In this place with no village ahead and no inn behind, in this ground crowded with tombstones of every size, he spun around. Guest-room doors surrounded him on all sides, spinning like a revolving lantern. Behind which door would he find the woman who had called for help?

Life is the same. When faced with countless choices, only one truly belongs to you. In this vast world of luxury and temptation, every life follows its own destiny, yet most people live only for their private desires. Who is more exhausted in the end—the body and heart, or the money?

He exhaled, then suddenly kicked open a door. The moment it burst inward a cold wind rushed out. Waves of chill turned into drifting shadows that wandered freely through the entire house. In the corner a person lay askew. When he drew closer he saw that she was already dead.

Because of his hesitation he had indirectly killed her…

He crouched down. His eyes reddened without his permission. He could not persuade himself not to grieve—yet why should he grieve? They were strangers. Would you feel such sorrow for the accidental death of a stranger?

### Character Notes

**Luo Juexing**  
The waking-world restraint (choosing the guest room, insisting on distance until the relationship is “official”) stands in sharp contrast to the dream, where hesitation itself becomes fatal. The question that closes the chapter—why a stranger’s death wounds him—points directly toward unresolved guilt from five years earlier.

**Qingmu Chengzi (Orange)**  
Her decision to trust him without a full explanation, followed by the quiet act of covering him and falling asleep while holding his hand, shows the depth of the bond that has formed. She enters the same dream without yet knowing it.

**The Shared Dream**  
A 1970s house that transforms into a mass grave, white silk, a single cry for help, and a death caused by hesitation. The imagery is dense with the themes that have followed Juexing since Li Mei: the cost of principle, the weight of delayed action, and the impossibility of remaining a pure observer.

**The Principle**  
The recurring line—“this is the principle by which you live”—echoes earlier chapters and Granny Luo Shen’s advice. In the dream it becomes both motivation and accusation.
