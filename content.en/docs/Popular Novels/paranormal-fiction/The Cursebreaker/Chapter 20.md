Forgetting does not mean something never happened. Memory works that way—rooted deep, impossible to uproot, like a nightmare that refuses to fade.

She sat on the edge of the midpoint platform, swinging her legs, and softly sang the song her father had written:

“Red-tiled roof, gray brick walls,  
beneath the wall a little girl;  
green grass, blue sea,  
on the sea a little boat…  
swaying with hope,  
coming home to cook…”

The melody was delicate, clear, and steeped in sorrow—the deep longing and bitterness of a grieving daughter.

There had once been a man who rose before dawn and labored until dark to protect the small plot of ancestral land left by his forebears. Through years of toil he turned a low single-story house into a proper two-story home with a roof and a foundation. Happiness lasted less than two years. Then, a year ago, a greater power crushed that life into nothing.

He refused to surrender the place that belonged to him, even unto death. He hanged himself, using his own life to defend his home. Yet his death brought none of the result he had hoped for. So the one person closest to him—his daughter—took up the sacred duty.

Heaven, she had learned, was never fair. Justice was nothing more than empty words on paper. Twice she appealed; twice she was rejected. She fell into darker hands and nearly lost her life. In the end she lay down in the place that ended her existence—this very ground.

Yang Xueqing’s eyes held only desolation. Sorrowful tears struck her skirt, darkening the blue fabric into something more vivid.

“No one is coming to save you,” she said. “You will pay for everything you have done.”

“Why me?” Song Xi struggled uselessly against the ropes. “What about Tang Gufeng? Why aren’t you going after him?”

“In your eyes other people’s lives have never been as important as your own. You really are scum.”

“Yes! I’m scum—worse than a dog or a pig! Just let me go and you can curse me however you want. Curse my ancestors for eighteen generations—I don’t care!”

She smiled and shook her head.

“To be this afraid of dying… you’ve reached the end. I only wonder whether your ancestors will be so angry they crawl out of their graves.”

Song Xi fell silent. In the distance he heard the approaching roar of a train. Was he truly about to die on these rails?

He began to thrash with everything he had. Skin tore on his arms and calves until blood ran, but pain had already lost meaning. The train’s headlight advanced like a countdown to death, steady and merciless.

“Help me—I don’t want to die young!”

His screams echoed across an empty station. No one came. Perhaps only then did he truly understand the meaning of the old saying: the world grows colder, and human hearts grow harder.

“Shut up.”

Yang Xueqing stood and slapped him. The sound seemed to vanish halfway, as if the night itself had swallowed it.

She did not leave. She simply stood and watched the express train 5120 draw closer.

Song Xi stopped struggling. He turned his face and stared at the oncoming light with pure despair. In those final moments every hope dissolved. Only then did he grasp the full cruelty and fragility of human nature. In the space of a heartbeat he seemed to understand everything—the sin he had committed, the father driven to suicide by his schemes, the girl whose body and spirit he had destroyed until she vanished. He had never known that even after death she had continued to guard this land without leaving.

Song Xi smiled. The smile washed the viciousness from his eyes. Then he wept—hating himself for worshipping money and power so completely that, in the end, both had destroyed him.

Two figures approached through the drifting joss paper. Faintly, the sound of a harmonica drifted on the air. Yang Xueqing turned, closed her eyes, and listened to the familiar melody. For a moment it carried her back to a time when she still had a home, love, and a father.

“Red-tiled roof, gray brick walls,  
beneath the wall a little girl…”

She began to hum along.

When she opened her eyes she saw the two men standing amid the swirling spirit money—one a police officer, the other a cursebreaker.

“Heaven gave you life,” Jiaye said, “but it did not give you the right to take the lives of others.”

“And so what?” she answered. “Do you believe you can still stop a story that is already ending?”

Shang Xuan continued to play the harmonica, completely absorbed. The sound felt strangely familiar to her.

“Your life was full of hardship—that is true. But those things have already become fact. What you are doing will not bring you joy. It will only bind you to sorrow for eternity.”

The music faded. Shang Xuan walked to her side and held out the harmonica. She saw, etched into the metal, the childish characters of her own name—carved in a playful moment long ago.

Her eyes flickered with confusion. Was it possible, as this man said, that she had not remained for the sake of revenge alone?

“We will build a grave for you and your father,” Shang Xuan continued, “so that you can keep watching over this land. Even though the place has become a symbol of prosperity, in your hearts it has never changed. You have already suffered enough under misfortune. Why force the ones who remain to keep suffering as well? You did nothing wrong. The stain placed on your pure hearts will one day be washed clean—so long as you continue to believe.”

He turned and pointed toward the station control room.

“This place will become your red-tiled roof and gray brick walls. It will stand as proof that you still exist. Song Xi has promised to come every Qingming Festival to pay his respects. He will keep that promise for the rest of his life. Give him the chance—and in doing so, give yourself final release.”

“Compensation?” Yang Xueqing’s voice was flat. “You know I have never believed in such things.”

“If time could turn back, no one would have wanted this ending,” Shang Xuan said.

“But time cannot turn back. Regret is useless. Do not waste your breath.”

Her decision was already made. She turned to face the oncoming train. At the exact moment Song Xi closed his eyes, the train vanished.

“What—?”

A figure shimmering with cyan light moved unsteadily onto the tracks, untied the ropes binding Song Xi, and pulled him clear. The train roared past in the space of a single blink.

Song Xi stared at the sky with empty, wooden eyes—as if his mind had simply stopped.

The cyan-glowing figure was Juexing. He walked to Yang Xueqing like a drifting green soul and held out a written pledge.

“You are a good child,” he said quietly. “No one should have carved a question mark into a heart as young as yours. I hope that when the sun rises tomorrow, you will be able to smile as you are released from below.”

She hesitated, then took the thin sheet of paper. It meant nothing to her on its own—yet the belief these three men insisted on holding was the same belief she had once carried herself.

Juexing opened his arms and drew her against his shoulder, gently stroking her long hair. The touch seemed to wash the exhaustion from her body. She began to weep softly. The care in his hand felt so much like her father’s that she could no longer refuse to forgive the wounds the world had given her.

Jiaye blocked the lens of Yu Xun’s camcorder with one hand. He could see the tears in the reporter’s eyes. She had never imagined she would witness a scene like this—perhaps only once in an entire lifetime.

The final punishment was forgiveness.

Every person carries a seed of kindness. No matter how ugly evil becomes, as long as a heart still knows how to forgive, black and white will not remain forever inverted.

She managed it.

Song Xi, however, was left simple—his mind broken clean through. Perhaps that was the kindest repayment possible: a life preserved so that he could still keep a promise every Qingming.

The sky cleared. The morning sun rose.

Jiaye found the dark-skinned man—the last person who had seen Yang Xueqing alive, and the one who had watched everything the night before from beside the fire basin.

“It’s over,” the man said, tear tracks cutting through the dust on his face. “She forgave everyone who was left.”

“Can you tell me what happened back then?” Jiaye took out paper and pen.

“When they first started building the tracks she ran onto the construction site. She kowtowed to every single worker, begging them not to take the land. Her forehead was already bleeding, but she kept knocking it against the ground. Watching her… it hurt more than I can say.”

Fresh tears fell.

After negotiating with the transportation bureau, Jiaye ordered excavation at the exact spot the man described. Beneath the platform of the midpoint station they found Yang Xueqing’s bones.

The lights in the operating room had burned for a full day and night before finally going dark. Orange wiped the tears from her cheeks and ran to the surgeon.

“How is he?”

The doctor’s expression was grave. He shook his head.

“We removed the bullet, but it damaged several internal organs. He has not yet passed the critical period.”

“Doctor—no matter how much it costs, you have to save him. I’m begging you.” Her grip on the white coat loosened. The look in the surgeon’s eyes was so bleak that she staggered backward and nearly fell.

“We will do everything we can. But if he does not make it through tonight…”

“Can I see him?”

The doctor nodded and left with the nurses.

Juexing lay still on the hospital bed. A single tear of quiet relief slipped from the corner of his eye, unseen by anyone. Orange dragged herself to the bedside. The monitors beeped in steady rhythm—proof that life still clung to him.

She sat down, took his still-warm hand in both of hers, and held on. Her mind was chaos. What she feared most was that he would never open his eyes again. If waiting was required, she would give her own life in exchange—just so that ten days from now he might wake.

### Character Notes

**Yang Xueqing**  
The arc of her revenge ends not in blood but in release. The childhood harmonica, the written pledge, and Juexing’s father-like embrace allow her to lay down the hatred that has bound her to the rails. Forgiveness becomes the true final act of strength.

**Luo Juexing**  
Even while critically wounded and burning with cyan light, he reaches the tracks in time to save Song Xi and offer the ghost the one thing she never received in life: gentle recognition that she was a good child. His sacrifice and compassion close the circle.

**Shang Xuan**  
Plays the decisive emotional card—the harmonica that still carries her childhood name. His words about building a lasting place of remembrance and the promise of Qingming visits provide the practical path out of endless vengeance.

**Song Xi**  
Survives physically but is left mentally shattered. The man who once valued only money and power is reduced to a broken vessel capable of little more than keeping a single annual promise.

**Qingmu Chengzi (Orange)**  
Ends the chapter in the quietest and most painful vigil—holding the hand of the man who took a bullet for her, ready to bargain her own life for his recovery.

**The Dark-Skinned Man**  
The final living witness. His testimony leads directly to the recovery of Yang Xueqing’s remains, giving her body the rest her spirit has finally accepted.
